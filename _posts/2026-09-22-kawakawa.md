<li>Botanical Name: <em>Piper excelsum</em></li>
<li>Habitat: lowland and coastal forests, often in shaded areas</li>

I grew up in a city in China where nature was not easily accessible. Most people lived in high-rise buildings, separated from their neighbours by just one wall. Even those interested in plants could usually only keep indoor pots, utilising whatever space they had in e.g. living rooms or balconies. For a long time, plants were not something that was always present in my daily life.

Later, when I came to New Zealand, in the middle of a bright summer, I started to be surrounded by many different kinds of plants. The first plants that struck me with awe were the luxuriant hydrangeas growing outside the villa-like UniFlat office in Dunedin - when I stopped by on the first evening, straight from the airport, to pick up the key to my accommodation - which also reminded me that I was in a different country.

During my four years in Dunedin, I always lived near the Botanic Garden: initially on Dundas Street, only a few metres away from the lower garden entrance, and later in North East Valley, where I walked through the garden to uni every day, passing a small herb garden on the way.

It was a small herb garden, yet well-maintained and organised, containing quite a variety considering its size. Each plant had an identification label with its scientific name, common name, and an indicator of its uses in e.g. cosmetics, medicine, or cuisine. Sweetly, visitors were welcome to pick a small amount, so sometimes, as I walked by, I would take some culinary herbs to add to meals: basil, mint, thyme, rosemary, chives, etc., and once I picked some fennel to make dumplings with a friend. And a few hundred metres away from the herb garden was a supermarket, where herbs like rosemary could be priced quite expensively. I used to wonder if people bought from supermarkets simply because they missed this gem in the Botanic Garden. I'm still not sure, but we humans can indeed be good at being selective in what we want to see, blind to things that don't catch our interest.

Though I spent a lot of time in the Dunedin Botanic Garden, running up and down its many corners, and certainly had a clear sense of what it was like in different seasons and weather, I was never good at plant identification. Partly because I was less interested in plant morphology than in their underlying biochemistry, partly because I lacked experience growing them, and partly because I was too busy with studies and other things at the time. Nevertheless, compared to when I was in China, I did start to have more connections with plants.

Later, I met several Kiwis and sometimes had the chance to visit their gardens. I started to appreciate the importance of gardens in Kiwis' lifestyles. People love plants for different reasons: some grew up in academic families and were influenced by parents who knew a lot about plants; some like their beautiful forms, colours, and aromas, and how plants keep their backyards gorgeous; and some enjoy the freshness and health benefits brought by home-grown food and choose to care for plants as a lifestyle choice. For me, it's more for sensory (the fresh ones do taste better) and curiosity reasons, so I would probably prefer an edible herb garden.

Kawakawa is the only Māori medicinal plant I can identify at the moment. As one herbalist I met during a medicinal plant workshop said, it is the most easily recognised medicinal herb in the bush. Which is true, and should thanks to its distinctive heart-shaped leaves. Once noticed, it is hard to miss when it is there.

I got my first few fresh Kawakawa leaves from a friend's backyard and when I followed the tips I found to make tea, I did quickly feel warmth inside my body after drinking it. Since I do not suffer from any conditions that Kawakawa known to be good for, such as skin complaints and wounds, toothache, kidney problems, so far, the only noticeable change I have experienced is its stimulation of blood circulation—slight warmth and facial flushing. That effect was strongest when I used the fresh Kawakawa leaves, picked just a few hours before brewing.

As a native plant, Kawakawa is also quite abundant. I’ve found it in several places I've hiked, including Tiritiri Matangi Island, Te Rau Puriri Regional Park and Mangemangeroa Reserve. And it is probably not surprising that I will keep bumping into it somewhere else in the future.

Earlier this year, when I was staying with a friend in Napier, she told me she had invited several friends over for a book-reading night. It was a cold night, so she lit the fireplace to keep the place warm. The doorbell rang several times, and each time a new guest showed up from the cold outside, they would put their bags on the floor and hang their coats near the fireplace to dry, rub their hands together, and try to find a place to sit down in the already-crowded living room. Every time a person came, my friend would ask what tea they'd like, and one option was always Kawakawa. Since I had also tried Kawakawa before, I knew that was quite a considerate offer to help guests warm up quickly, particularly suited to that day's weather. Guests probably would not feel it the way I did, but as a bit of a nerdy person, I think that's what knowledge of plants can give us: it helps us choose the right thing at the right time, for the right purpose, so that everything falls into the right place.

And today when making the Kawakawa tea in the kitchen, it suddenly came to my mind: why did I not see Kawakawa in Dunedin Botanic Garden before. Well,  Google said it was actually there, in the Native Plant collection parallel to Lovelock avenue, a place that I definitely had been to countless times before. But just like those people who might not pay enough attention to that small herb garden, I also overlooked the Kawakawa tree back then, it was just invisible to me. Once again then, what we can see in the world is determined by what we know and what interests us, but no matter whether or not we find plants interesting, Those plants will always quietly be there and whisper in ways that only those who are willing to listen can understand.
